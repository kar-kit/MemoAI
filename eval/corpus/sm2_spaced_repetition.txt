Lecture 4: Spaced Repetition and the SM-2 Algorithm

The forgetting curve
Hermann Ebbinghaus studied his own memory of nonsense syllables in the 1880s. He found that recall drops quickly in the first day after learning and then declines more slowly. This pattern is called the forgetting curve. Reviewing material just before it would be forgotten flattens the curve, and each successful review lets the next review be scheduled further in the future. This idea is called spaced repetition.

The spacing effect
The spacing effect is the finding that study sessions spread out over time produce better long-term retention than the same total study time massed into one session. Cramming the night before an exam can support short-term recall but tends to produce poor retention weeks later.

SuperMemo and SM-2
Piotr Wozniak developed the SuperMemo family of algorithms. SM-2, published in 1987, is the version most widely copied by flashcard software, including early versions of Anki. SM-2 keeps three numbers for every card: the repetition count n, the ease factor EF, and the inter-repetition interval I measured in days.

Grading a review
After each review the learner grades their recall quality q on a scale from 0 to 5. A grade of 5 means a perfect response. A grade of 3 means a correct response recalled with serious difficulty. Grades 0 to 2 mean the answer was wrong or forgotten.

Updating the interval
If q is 3 or higher the response counts as correct. On the first correct repetition the interval is set to 1 day. On the second it is set to 6 days. After that the new interval is the previous interval multiplied by the ease factor, rounded. If q is below 3 the repetition count is reset to 0 and the interval goes back to 1 day, so the card is relearned from the start, but the ease factor is kept.

Updating the ease factor
The ease factor starts at 2.5 for a new card. After each review it is updated with the formula EF' = EF + (0.1 - (5 - q) * (0.08 + (5 - q) * 0.02)). A grade of 5 raises EF by 0.1, a grade of 4 leaves it unchanged, and a grade of 3 lowers it by 0.14. The ease factor is never allowed to fall below 1.3, because intervals that grow too slowly make the learner see hard cards too often.

Limitations
SM-2 treats every card independently and does not model how difficult a card is relative to other cards. Newer schedulers such as FSRS fit a memory model to a learner's review history and predict the probability of recall directly.
